A good AI answers almost any question, one question at a time. But the work of a department is rarely one question. A report, a hiring round or an offer needs several kinds of knowledge, done in the right order and checked before it goes out.

In a company, that kind of work goes to a team. One person knows the numbers, another knows the client, a third checks. AI can work the same way: instead of one assistant that does everything, you set up a team of AI experts (what the industry calls AI agents). Each has its own role and instructions, and can have its own information and tools: your documents, a data table, a web search. Then you decide how they work together.

There are five ways, much like the ways human teams work: one after another, all at the same time, by passing the case to the right colleague, by discussing in turns, or under a manager who decides who works next. The way you choose matters as much as the experts themselves, because it decides who speaks to whom, in what order, and who has the last word. This guide explains each of the five, with an everyday comparison, what it is good at, and where it goes wrong.

## Why does the way of working matter?

Because a group of capable specialists is not yet a team.

Put five good people in a room without telling them who starts, who checks and who decides, and you get repeated work and contradictory answers. AI experts are no different. The same three experts can produce a clean result or a muddle, depending only on how they are organised.

## The five at a glance

| Way of working | Like | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sequential | An assembly line | Steps that always come in the same order | One weak step spoils everything after it |
| Concurrent | Colleagues each taking a file | Independent tasks, when speed matters | Results that do not fit together |
| Handoff | A reception desk | Requests that must find the right specialist | Cases passed around without an owner |
| Group chat | A meeting | Questions that gain from being challenged | Long discussions that cost a lot |
| Manager-led | A project lead and a crew | Jobs where the steps depend on the request | A manager with a vague brief |

## 1. Sequential: one after another

<figure>
<img src="/media/blog/five-ways/line.png" alt="In a line: a researcher, a writer and a reviewer, each building on the one before." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

Each expert finishes its part and passes the result to the next. Research, then draft, then review.

**Like:** an assembly line. Each station does one thing to what the previous one produced.

**Good at:** processes where the order never changes. It is predictable, and when the result is wrong you can see at which step it went wrong. It is the simplest way, and a good first choice.

**Watch out for:** a mistake early in the chain travels all the way down. Put a checking step where an error would be expensive.

**Example:** a researcher gathers facts on a competitor, a writer turns them into a one-page note, a reviewer checks the note against the facts.

## 2. Concurrent: all at the same time

<figure>
<img src="/media/blog/five-ways/once.png" alt="All at once: the brief goes to a legal, a costs and a risks expert at the same time, and their views are gathered in one report." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

All the experts start together, each on its own part or from its own angle, and the results are gathered at the end.

**Like:** five colleagues who each take one file from the pile and meet again when they are done.

**Good at:** tasks that do not depend on each other. Reading five sources, checking a document against five rules, looking at one question from five angles. It is the fastest way to cover ground.

**Watch out for:** nobody sees what the others are doing while they work. If the parts need to agree with each other, add a member that puts them together and resolves the differences.

**Example:** three experts study the same contract, one for the legal side, one for the costs, one for the risks. Or one expert per store reads that store's pages at the same moment, then the offers are compared.

## 3. Handoff: passing the case to the right colleague

<figure>
<img src="/media/blog/five-ways/handoff.png" alt="Passing the case on: a front desk sends the request to billing rather than to technical support." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

One expert is active at a time. An expert that receives a request it should not handle passes it to the colleague who should. You can decide in advance who is allowed to pass a case to whom.

**Like:** a reception desk. You explain why you came, and you are sent to the right department, which may send you on if needed.

**Good at:** requests that arrive mixed. A customer message could be about a delivery, an invoice or a technical fault, and each needs a different specialist.

**Watch out for:** a case that keeps being passed along. Give every case a clear owner, and limit who can hand over to whom.

**Example:** a first expert reads each incoming request and sends it to billing, delivery or technical support.

## 4. Group chat: a discussion in turns

<figure>
<img src="/media/blog/five-ways/chat.png" alt="One conversation: a writer, a critic and an editor take turns and each reads what the others said." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

The experts speak in turns in one shared conversation, and every one of them sees everything that was said. Each reacts to the others.

**Like:** a meeting where everyone hears everyone.

**Good at:** questions with no single right answer, where being challenged improves the result. Reviewing a plan, weighing a decision, improving a piece of writing. Not for work with a clear sequence, or with one precise thing to deliver.

**Watch out for:** meetings can run long. More turns mean more calls to AI models, so more time and cost. Set the maximum number of turns.

**Example:** a writer and a critic improve a text over a few turns. Or a strategist proposes a campaign, a finance specialist challenges the budget, and a brand specialist checks the tone, until the plan holds.

## 5. Manager-led: a manager decides who works next

<figure>
<img src="/media/blog/five-ways/manager.png" alt="Under a manager: the manager sends the hotel expert to work, while the flights and transfers experts wait their turn." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

A manager supervises the work. It sends the request to one specialist at a time, reads the result, then decides who works next or gives the final answer.

**Like:** a project lead with a crew. The lead does not do the work, but knows who should.

**Good at:** jobs with many parts, where no two requests need exactly the same steps. The manager adapts the plan to each request, and calls only on the specialists it needs.

**Watch out for:** everything rests on the manager's brief. If its role is vague, it will hand out work badly. Describe carefully what each specialist is for.

**Example:** the [Trip Planner team](/use-cases/trip-planner) has a manager and twelve specialists: flights, hotel, transfers and more. A weekend away and a month abroad do not call on the same ones.

## How do you choose?

Match the shape to the work. Answer three questions about the job.

**Do the steps always come in the same order?** If yes, go sequential. If the order depends on the request, look at manager-led or handoff.

**Do the parts depend on each other?** If not, run them concurrently and save time. If they do, they must happen in order, or be discussed.

**Is there one right answer, or does the answer gain from debate?** If debate helps, use a group chat, with a maximum number of turns.

When two ways seem to fit, pick the simpler one. You can change later: the members keep their roles, and only the organisation changes.

## Can you combine them? A small AI company

<figure>
<img src="/media/blog/five-ways/company.png" alt="A small AI company: a director sends work to a marketing team, a sales team and an operations team, each with its own experts." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

Yes, and real teams often do. A member of a team does not have to be an expert: it can be another team.

A director can send the work to a marketing team, a sales team or an operations team, and each of them has its own experts and its own way of working. A manager can hand "write the report" to a small sequential team of writer and reviewer, and "check the sources" to three experts working concurrently. You build large solutions from small parts you have already tested.

Start simple all the same. A team of three working sequentially teaches you more about your job than a large structure you cannot follow.

## Where do people fit in?

<figure>
<img src="/media/blog/five-ways/human.png" alt="The team prepares, a person says yes, and only then does the work go further." width="1200" height="675" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

In all five. A team of AI experts does not have to work alone: the team prepares, your people review and decide. Whatever the way of working, the work should stop and ask when a decision belongs to a person: approving a rejection, choosing between two options, confirming a payment. You decide where those stops go, and you can see what each expert received and produced. Where to put them is a separate decision, covered in [where a person should stay in charge](/blog/human-in-the-loop-ai).
